What is green noise?

Written by the team that builds Luten, so we are not neutral about it. Updated 6 November 2026.

The short answer

Green noise is a loose name for steady noise with extra level around the middle of the frequency range. It has no fixed definition: we found no standard for it, and the sources below place it anywhere from around 500 Hz to about 2,900 Hz. Wikipedia has a short clip labelled green noise that you can play. To make your own, you can try the Equalizer in Background Sounds on iPhone, or Audacity on a computer. Luten has nothing labelled green noise.

What the name means

Most noise colours are borrowed from light. Wikipedia says the habit began with white noise, and that other colours followed, often because the spectrum resembles that colour of light. Brown is the exception: it is named after Brownian motion.

So nothing about the sound is green. The name describes where the level sits across frequencies. Frequency is heard as pitch and measured in hertz (Hz): low numbers are rumble and high numbers are hiss. In nearly every use we read, green means somewhere around the middle.

Does green noise have a fixed definition?

No. Wikipedia files it under informal names. It says the International Electrotechnical Commission defines white and pink noise, and US Federal Standard 1037C defines white, pink, blue and black. Green isn't in either list as Wikipedia gives them. Here is who says what.

  • Joseph S. Wisniewski, in a 1996 Usenet post. His FAQ says it was defined by "some folks" producing tapes, and calls it, supposedly, "the background noise of the world": a long-term average of the spectrum at outdoor sites. He describes it as close to pink noise with extra level around 500 Hz, then asks if anyone has the actual spectrum.
  • J. Russell Lemon, in a comment printed with that FAQ. He recalls a US government spec for testing communication channels, flat from 500 Hz to about 2 kHz. He is going from memory, and our search found no copy of it.
  • Daniel Lau, Gonzalo Arce and Neal Gallagher, in a 1998 paper in Proceedings of the IEEE. They define green noise as the mid-frequency part of white noise. Their subject is dot patterns for printing images, not sound.
  • John D. Cook, a consultant in mathematics and statistics, on his blog in 2016. He quotes a source saying these colours aren't rigorously defined. Mapping the rainbow onto the range of hearing, he gets about 1612 to 2919 Hz, and says that is his reading of a vague description.
  • Wikipedia also lists bounded Brownian noise as a meaning, with no source given.

Wikipedia's 500 Hz comes from Wisniewski's FAQ. Cook's range starts more than three times higher. Read any exact figure as one author's choice.

Green noise vs white, pink and brown noise

White, pink and brown are each described by one slope across the whole range. Green is described by a region instead.

  • White: flat. The same power per hertz all the way up.
  • Pink: falls about 3 decibels per octave as the pitch rises.
  • Brown: falls about 6 decibels per octave.
  • Green: no agreed slope. Extra level, or all of the level, sits in a band around the middle, depending on whose version it is.

Two files made to the definition of brown noise should measure alike. Two files called green noise may not. See our page on white, pink and brown noise for those colours.

Where to find or make green noise

None of these is the official version, because there isn't one.

Hear a sample on Wikipedia

The Wikipedia article has a short clip labelled as Wisniewski's version. The file is marked public domain, and the uploader doesn't say how it was made.

On iPhone: Background Sounds with the Equalizer

The Apple pages we read don't use the name green noise. They describe an Equalizer that changes the levels of chosen frequencies, on iOS 26 or later. Apple's steps come to this:

  1. Open Settings, tap Accessibility, tap Audio & Visual, tap Background Sounds, then turn it on.
  2. Tap the current sound and choose a noise. Apple lists balanced, bright and dark noise.
  3. Tap Equalizer and turn it on.
  4. Drag across the Tone grid to pick a range of frequencies. Broad covers wider ranges and Focused more specific ones.

Apple's pages don't say where the middle is on the grid, and they don't describe this as a way to make green noise, so go by ear.

On a computer: make it in Audacity

Audacity says it is free and open source, and runs on Windows, macOS and Linux. Its noise generator offers white, pink and Brownian, so green is up to you. These steps follow Wisniewski's description.

  1. Choose Generate, then Noise. Set Type to Pink, and Amplitude well below the default of 0.8, because the next step adds level. Set Duration to the file length you want.
  2. With the noise selected, choose Effect, then EQ and filters, then Filter Curve EQ.
  3. Click the curve near 500 Hz to add a point and drag it up, with a point each side to keep the hump gentle. No height is agreed, so go by ear.
  4. Choose File, then Export audio to save an ordinary audio file.

Turn your volume down before you press play.

On Android

A search of Google's help pages turned up nothing on green noise when we looked, so we can't point to a built-in way. Use the clip, a file you made, or a video or app.

Videos and apps

YouTube, the App Store and Google Play all have videos or apps using the name. The label is the maker's, and the titles we looked at don't say which version they mean. Judge by ear.

Is there green noise in Luten?

Luten has nothing labelled green noise. The noise colours it names are white, pink and brown, and we don't claim any of them is green. If you want green noise by name, use one of the ways above.

Questions

Is green noise real?

The name is real and widely used. What is missing is one agreed definition, so two people can mean different sounds by it.

What does green noise sound like?

There is no one answer, because versions differ. Wikipedia has a short clip labelled green noise that you can play and judge for yourself.

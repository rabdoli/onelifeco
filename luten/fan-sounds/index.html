Fan sounds for sleeping, without the fan

If you have slept with a fan on for years, you may never have asked what is in the sound. It is worth knowing, because it tells you what to look for when you want the sound without the draught.

The short answer: there is nothing in it. That sounds like a joke, and it is the whole description.

Why a fan is steady and rain is not

A fan is the same every second. No drops, no gusts, no melody, nothing that begins or ends. There is no event in it, nothing to follow and nothing to wait for.

Rain is not like that. Rain is broadband noise with texture: individual drops, density that changes, the odd gust across the window. Waves have a rhythm. Anything with a tempo has a next beat coming. Some people like that movement. Others keep noticing it and would rather not.

Neither reaction is a fault.

A fan is not white noise, though people mix them up

This is the most useful thing on this page if white noise has ever felt harsh to you.

White noise carries equal energy at every frequency, including the very top of your hearing, which is why it reads as bright and hissy, like a radio between stations. A fan is band limited. The motor and the blades put out a lot of low and mid energy and very little of that top end, and a wall or a door takes off more again.

So the sound someone has in mind when they say "white noise" may be closer to a fan than to actual white noise. If you have tried a white noise track and found it sharp, you very probably did not dislike noise. You disliked the top end. A fan, or pink or brown noise, is the same idea with that part taken off.

The ceiling fan problem

People talk about "a fan" as though it were one sound. The single biggest split is not the size of the fan, it is whether the fan pulses.

A ceiling fan is a large slow blade passing over your head. Each pass gives a small rise and fall in the sound, and any fan that is slightly out of balance adds a wobble, a tick or a faint scrape on top of it. That is a repeating event, which a steady sound does not have.

Some people like that rhythm. Others notice it and start counting. If you have never liked a ceiling fan but you like a box fan, you are not imagining it. Try a sound with no pulse in it rather than a quieter fan.

Six fans that are six different sounds

A box fan on the floor

Broad, low, plenty of body, very little pulse. The steadiest of the real fans.

A ceiling fan

Airy and open, with the blade pass in it. The most divisive one, for the reason above.

A small fan running close by

A laptop or a desk fan. Higher pitched, narrower, one insistent note.

A fan in the next room

A wall and a doorway roll the top end off it and blur it. Softer and further away in a way that turning the volume down does not reproduce, because turning it down takes the low end off, not the high end.

Air through a vent or a duct

Air conditioning, extraction, forced air heating. Steadier than any bladed fan because you are hearing moving air rather than a rotating object, though a wide duct adds a resonance of its own.

An aircraft cabin

The largest version of the same thing. Enveloping and weighted toward the low end.

Why look for a recording at all, when you could just buy a fan

Almost never because of the sound. Because of the air.

  • A real fan cools the room, and half the year that is the opposite of what you want.
  • It blows air across the bed.
  • It moves the curtains, the blind cord and the paper on the desk.
  • Somebody else in the room may have an opinion about it.
  • It is not in the hotel room, on the plane, or in the office.

A recording gives the sound and none of that, and it goes where the fan does not.

Fan sounds for sleeping with a black screen

Type "fan sounds for sleeping" into a search box and the suggestions add "black screen", "no ads" and "youtube". The black screen is there to keep the room dark, but it is still the screen. What you want is the sound with the phone locked, which is a different thing.

1. YouTube with the screen off

YouTube's own help page says background play only works in the YouTube mobile apps and requires a YouTube Premium membership. The same page says some videos are not available for background play, so check the one you want.

2. The sounds already on your iPhone

Apple's Background Sounds are built into the phone. Open Settings, tap Accessibility, then Audio & Visual, then Background Sounds, and turn it on. Tap the sound to choose another. Apple's pages name ocean, rain and bright noise among them and do not name a fan, so try the noise sounds and judge for yourself. They keep playing with the phone locked unless you turn on Stop Sounds When Locked. On iOS 26 or later, Stop Sounds with a Timer on the same screen turns them off after a set time or at a set clock time. Full steps, and where Background Sounds stops.

3. An app that plays in the background

Press play, lock the phone, and check that the sound keeps going before you rely on it. On Android, battery savers on some phone brands can stop apps running in the background, so test it on your own phone. The loop problem is easier here, and there is still a catch

Fan noise is the easiest sound in the world to loop invisibly, because there is no memorable moment in it to hear come around a second time. Rain is full of recognisable gusts and runs of drops. A fan has none.

The catch is that a recording of a real fan is not only the fan. It also has the motor's rattle in it, a bearing tick, mains hum, the shape of the room it was recorded in, and whatever went past the window during the take. Those repeat. Once you have heard the same little tick twice, you are listening to the loop instead of the fan, and you cannot go back.

This is the argument for a fan sound that is built rather than captured: nothing gets in that was not put in.

How to test a fan track

1. Skip to the last ten seconds

That is where the join lives, and where an outro voice or a fade would be.

2. Listen for anything periodic

A tick, a wobble, a scrape, a rise and fall. If you can count it, you will notice it again.

3. Play it at the volume you would actually use

Quiet fan noise loses its low end first, so a track that felt warm and full can turn thin and hissy.

4. Leave it running and do something else

What sounds richest in the first four seconds is not always what you want left on.

5. Let a timer run out with the screen off

Some apps announce the next track, resume a queue, or throw an advertisement in when the thing you chose finishes. Better to find that out now than then.

What can be said about fan sounds

Fan sounds are not a treatment and nothing here is advice about a sleep problem. What can be said is about the sound:

  • Preferences vary enormously. Yours does not need to match anyone else's.
  • A steady fan has no events in it. That is a plain description of the sound, not a promise about what it will do for you.

Common questions

Why do people like fan sounds?

Because a fan has nothing in it. It is the same every second: no drops, no gusts, no melody, nothing that starts or stops. Rain, waves and anything with a tempo all have events in them. If you would rather not have those, a steadier sound may suit you.

Is a fan sound the same as white noise?

No, and this is the most useful thing to know if white noise has ever felt harsh to you. White noise carries equal energy at every frequency, including the very top, which is why it reads as bright and hissy. A fan is band limited: the motor and the blades produce a lot of low and mid energy and very little of that top end, and the room takes off more again. So a fan may be closer to what you picture when you say white noise than white noise itself is. If white noise feels sharp, a fan is usually the thing you actually wanted.

Why do some people dislike a ceiling fan?

Because a ceiling fan is not steady. A big slow blade passing overhead gives a regular rise and fall in the sound, and a slightly unbalanced one adds a wobble or a tick on top of that. That is a repeating event, which a steady sound does not have. Some people like the rhythm, and some notice it and start counting. A box fan, a vent or a fan heard from another room has far less of that pulse in it.

Why use a fan recording instead of a real fan?

Usually because of the air rather than the sound. A real fan cools the room, blows air across the bed, and is unwelcome in winter or when someone else is in the bed. It also moves the curtains and the papers on the desk. Someone who has slept with a fan for years may want the sound with none of that, and a recording is also the only version available in a hotel room, on a plane or in an office.

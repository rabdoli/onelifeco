Rain is easy to find and hard to find clean. Many versions have an ad, a thunder crack or a piano put into it.

The usual story: you search rain, you find a track, and partway through a thunder crack goes off, or a piano fades up, or a voice says something. You wanted rain, and now there is something else in it.

Rain sounds for sleeping with no ads

If you are searching from YouTube, a "no ads" title does not decide whether ads play. YouTube's own help page says that on monetized videos of 8 minutes or longer, ads can play in the middle of the video, and that automatic mid-roll ad slots are selected by default. A long rain video can carry them whatever its title says.

There are three ways round it.

1. The rain already on your iPhone

Apple's Background Sounds include rain, built into the phone, with no ads. Open Settings, tap Accessibility, then Audio & Visual, then Background Sounds, and turn it on. Tap the sound to choose another. On iOS 26 or later, a timer on the same screen can turn it off after a set time or at a set clock time. 2. YouTube Premium

YouTube says Premium removes the ads that play before and during a video. Its help page also says you may still see branding or promotions the creator built into the video itself, so anything said or shown inside the recording stays.

On Google Play, a listing shows a "Contains ads" label when the app has ads, so check for it before you install. On iPhone, read the listing, then play one rain track with the screen locked before you rely on it. Luten has no ads: One Life runs no third-party advertising in its apps.

Why thunder is in almost everything

Thunder is in rain tracks for the same reason every thumbnail is orange. A rumble reads as atmosphere in a two second preview, and a preview is what a track has to win on.

What it does later is the opposite. A thunder crack is a loud, sudden sound. Dropped into steady rain, it stands out.

The same argument covers the music. A pad or a piano under rain has a key and a tempo, and a tempo is a thing to follow. It also puts the track on a clock. Rain does not resolve. Music does, and you can feel it coming.

Why some people like rain and others do not

This is the part worth knowing before you decide rain is not for you, because the difference is in the sound.

A fan is the same every second. There is nothing in it to pick out, nothing to follow, nothing to wait for. Rain is not like that. Rain is broadband noise with texture: individual drops, density that changes, the odd gust across the window. There are events in it.

For a lot of people that texture is the whole appeal. Others keep noticing the events and would rather not. Neither reaction is a fault. If you are in the second group, try a steadier sound, which usually means a fan, or pink noise, or something else with no events in it at all.

The surface is most of the sound

People who dislike rain sounds often dislike one surface and have not met the others.

Rain on glass

Sharp, high pitched, distinct taps with a lot of top end. Close and detailed. The most recognisable version of rain and the most eventful.

Rain on canvas

A tent or an awning. Broader and duller, more body, fewer separate hits. This is the one people mean when they say they want rain but nothing too busy.

Rain on leaves and soil

The drops scatter across so many surfaces that they blur into a wash. The closest rain gets to plain broadband noise.

Rain on pavement, in a town

Between glass and canvas, and it usually brings splash and distant traffic with it. Cosy for some people, too much going on for others.

Rain heard from indoors, a room away

Muffled by a wall or a closed window, which rolls the top end off it. Quieter and softer at the same time, in a way that turning the volume down does not reproduce.

Rain and pink noise are close relatives, not the same thing

Steady heavy rain sits near pink noise, whose energy falls about 3 decibels per octave, which is why both sound fuller and more even than the bright hiss of white noise.

The difference is that pink noise is generated to a defined slope and holds still, while rain is thousands of small impacts and therefore wanders: it swells, thins, gusts and comes back. If you like rain but wish it would stop moving, pink noise is the next thing to try. If you find pink noise sterile, that movement is exactly what you are missing.

The loop problem is worse for rain than for anything else

Almost every rain track is a short recording repeated. That is fine until you hear a moment twice: a particular gust, a run of heavier drops, a car passing. Once you have heard it come around, you are waiting for it, and you are now listening to the loop instead of the rain.

Rain is the worst case for this because it is full of recognisable moments. Plain noise can loop badly and get away with it. Rain cannot.

How to test a rain track

1. Skip to the last ten seconds

That is where the join lives, and where an outro voice or a fade would be. It is the least tested part of any sound app and it tells you most of what you need to know.

2. Leave it running and do something else

Rain is not judged in the first four seconds. What sounds richest at the start is not always what you want left on.

3. Check the surface, not the title

"Rain" tells you nothing. Glass, canvas, leaves and pavement are four different sounds and a track called Rain could be any of them.

4. Play it at the volume you would actually use

Quiet rain loses its low end first, so a track that felt warm and full can turn thin and hissy.

What can be said about rain

Rain sounds are not a treatment and nothing here is advice for a sleep problem. What can be said is about the sound:

  • A track with no thunder, no music and no voice has fewer events in it, whatever the label on it says.

Why do most rain sounds have thunder in them?

Why do some people dislike rain sounds?

Some people like that. Others would rather have a steadier sound, such as a fan or pink noise. Neither reaction is unusual.

What is the difference between rain on a window and rain on a tent?

The surface, and the surface is most of the sound. Glass gives sharp high pitched taps with a lot of top end. Canvas gives a broader, duller patter with more body and fewer distinct events. Leaves and soil scatter the drops so much that the individual hits blur into something closer to a wash. Pavement sits between glass and canvas and usually carries traffic and splash with it. Do rain sounds loop?
